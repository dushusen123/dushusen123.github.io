What Is a Good MOQ for a New Clothing Brand?

Order too few and you pay a premium per unit. Order too many and you're sitting on inventory that expires faster than you can discount it. Here's the data on how many pieces to actually order first.

Why Factories Have MOQs at All

Minimum Order Quantity isn't a factory trying to block you out. It's a function of fixed costs that don't shrink when you order less. Every custom garment order has:

  • Pattern making & sample approval — the engineer's time is the same whether you order 50 or 5,000 pieces.
  • Fabric roll minimums — fabric mills sell by the roll (typically 25–35 meters for knits). Below that, you pay cut-piece prices or factory-retail fabric.
  • Cutting setup — laying out markers and aligning fabric takes hours, regardless of piece count.
  • Print / embroidery setup — screen print screens, digitizing, machine threading.
  • Machine changeover — operators get efficient after the first 50 pieces; below that they're still learning the garment.

Divide $1,500 of fixed costs by 100 pieces and it's $15/pc. Divide it by 1,000 pieces and it's $1.50/pc. That's the entire MOQ story. ["https://luopeitarx.com/blogs/article/why-50-piece-custom-t-shirt-orders-cost-more"]

The Stage-Based Framework

The right MOQ isn't one number — it's a sequence. Here's how serious brands actually scale:

Stage 1 — The Test Run (50–100 pcs per design)

Goal: Prove the factory can execute, not the market. Budget: ~$1,000–3,000. How many designs: 1–3. Colors: 1–2. Sizes: S–XL only (skip 2XL, it sells slow for new brands).

At this stage, you're paying a premium on purpose. You're buying information: can the factory hit the spec? Is the fabric right? Are the sizes what your tech pack said? Treat every piece as R&D.

Stage 2 — The Pilot (300–500 pcs per design)

Goal: Prove the product sells. Budget: ~$5,000–10,000. How many designs: 2–5. Colors: 2–3. Sizes: Full S–XXL range.

This is where you start getting real bulk pricing. Unit cost drops 30–40% versus the 50-piece price. You should only do this stage after the test run sold through at 70%+ — not before.

Stage 3 — The Reorder (1,000+ pcs per design)

Goal: Meet proven demand. Budget: ~$10,000+. At this point fabric mills quote "bulk rates," machine efficiency hits 90%, and per-unit cost drops another 12–22% versus 500 pieces. ["https://apparel.alibaba.com/guide/t-shirt-production-cost-guide-real-2026-breakdown"]

The 500-piece order saves $900 on unit cost, but locks $3,870 of your capital in unsold stock — 4.3x more. For a brand doing under $100k in annual revenue, that $3,870 is the difference between buying your next fabric sample and shutting the store down. Industry data shows many early-stage clothing brands fail because inventory costs trap them before they reach their first 500 customers. The low-MOQ approach requires roughly 80% less startup capital than the standard bulk approach while accepting only a modest per-unit premium — a trade-off that's almost always worth it for brands that haven't proven repeat demand yet. Three MOQ Myths to Ignore

Myth 1: "MOQ 1 means I can try the factory risk-free"

No. A "MOQ 1" listing on Alibaba is almost always a stock tee they're reselling, not true OEM. You get their blank, your print on it, and no control over fabric, fit or labels. For a brand, that's not manufacturing — that's print-on-demand with extra steps.

Myth 2: "Lower MOQ is always better"

Below 50 pieces, the per-unit cost premium becomes punitive — often 150%+ over bulk. You're paying the factory to essentially run samples. If you can't sell 50 pieces at a price that covers that premium, you shouldn't be manufacturing at all. Use POD or DTG for true micro-runs.

Myth 3: "I should order my whole collection at once"

Ordering 8 designs at 100 pieces each = 800 pieces, but each design is too small to be profitable. Order 3 designs at 200 pieces each = 600 pieces, but each one has enough volume to make the premium tolerable. Concentrate, don't scatter.

How to Negotiate MOQ

Factories quote a standard MOQ (often 500 or 1,000) because that's their default. But MOQ is negotiable — here's what actually moves the number:

  1. Offer a deposit + reorder commitment. "I'll order 100 now and reorder 400 in 60 days if it sells" gets you flexibility on the first order.
  2. Consolidate across designs. 100 pieces across one design is hard. 300 pieces across three of their existing fabric/fit bases is easy — the factory already has the pattern.
  3. Use their stock fabric. Custom dyeing triggers a fabric mill MOQ. Using their existing white/black/natural stock color removes that constraint.
  4. Pay on time, every time. Factories remember the brands that don't delay their 70% balance. Repeat, on-time customers get the flexible MOQ — first-timers get the standard number.

Our Recommendation

For most new brands reading this, our answer is simple:

Start at 100 pieces per design, 2–3 designs, with a factory that can scale to 500 and 1,000 on reorder.

That's exactly why Dadu Du Clothing built our MOQ at 100 pcs per design. It's small enough to test your market without gambling your operating capital, but large enough that you're paying real OEM pricing &mdash> not sample-shop prices — and your factory relationship starts on footing that lets you reorder at 500, 1,000 or 5,000 without renegotiating everything.

Don't confuse low MOQ with low quality. The factories that survive at 100-piece MOQs are the ones whose process is tight enough that the fixed cost burden per unit doesn't crush them. That's the factory you want to grow with.
