What Is AQL 2.5 & Why It Matters for Your Order

AQL 2.5 is the number big brands use to decide whether a container of t-shirts ships back to you or gets rejected. It's also the most misunderstood term in apparel sourcing. Here's what it actually means.

AQL in One Sentence

AQL (Acceptable Quality Limit, per ISO 2859-1 / ANSI Z1.4) is a statistical sampling standard: instead of inspecting all 1,000 pieces in your order, the inspector pulls a random sample (typically 80 pieces for a 1,000-unit lot) and counts defects. If the sample has too many, the whole shipment fails. ["https://www.sleekapparels.com/blog/aql-25-explained/"]

The "2.5" means: the batch passes only if the sampled defect rate is statistically consistent with no more than 2.5% major defects.

The Three Defect Classes

ClassAQL limitExamplesResult if exceeded
Critical0Broken needle inside fabric, mold, sharp edges, wrong materialSingle found = whole shipment rejected
Major2.5%Holes, open seams, wrong color, crooked print, size out of tolerance (>2cm), missing labelShipment rejected; 100% inspection at supplier cost
Minor4.0%Loose thread, slight stain, small stitching irregularity that doesn't affect saleBuyer can accept or negotiate discount

Sources: Alibaba Seller Central AQL Standards Guide 2026; Topology Apparel QC Guide 2026; HQTS AQL 2.5 Reference. How Many Pieces Actually Get Checked?

The sample size comes from a lookup table based on your total lot size:

Lot sizeSample inspected (General II)Max major defects to passMax minor defects to pass
500 pcs50 pcs37
1,000 pcs80 pcs57
2,000 pcs125 pcs710
5,000 pcs200 pcs1014

Source: ISO 2859-1 / ANSI Z1.4 General Inspection Level II. ["https://scanerp.pro/blog/aql-sampling-garment-inspection-iso-2859.html","https://www.textileindustry.net/aql-chart-for-sampling-acceptance-level/"]

So for a 1,000-piece order, the inspector checks 80 pieces. If they find 6 or more major defects, the whole shipment fails. That's rigorous — and that's why serious factories build in-process checks, not just final QC.

What This Means For You as a Buyer

If a supplier says "we do 100% inspection" — ask if that means AQL 2.5 or just eyeballing. Real AQL inspections follow the ISO table, measure dimensions, check fabric, print and labeling systematically. "We check everything" with no documented process is usually a worker folding t-shirts and hoping.

Ask for the report. A real inspection (whether by the factory, QIMA, SGS or Bureau Veritas) produces a PDF with photos, measurements, defect list and a pass/fail stamp. If your supplier can't send one before shipment, you're the one inspecting at your own warehouse — with no leverage after payment.

Premium brands tighten to AQL 1.5. Luxury and designer programs use a stricter 1.5 major-defect limit. You can ask for this, but it usually adds 10–15% to the unit cost because the factory has to rework more pieces. ["https://www.sleekapparels.com/blog/aql-25-explained/"]

What We Do at Dadu Du

Our QC process runs in five stages, not just at the end:

  1. Fabric incoming — GSM, color shade, shrinkage test before cutting.
  2. Cutting inspection — panel size, fabric defects, marker alignment.
  3. Sewing in-line — line leader checks every 50 pieces for stitch quality and dimension.
  4. Print / embroidery — first piece approved, then random check through the run.
  5. Final AQL 2.5 inspection — full audit with photo report before goods ship.

You receive the final inspection report with photos. If your 1,000-piece order fails AQL 2.5, we fix it — not you.

One Thing to Remember

AQL is not "2.5% of your order is allowed to be defective." It's a statistical test: it means the factory's process is good enough that a random sample should usually pass. A 2.5% actual defect rate in a finished shipment is still 25 bad pieces per 1,000 — and that's exactly why we check at five stages instead of one.
