How to Choose a Custom T-Shirt Manufacturer in China (2026)

China still makes one in every three garments worn on the planet. But "a factory in China" ranges from a 500-worker plant with a BV audit report to a five-person trading company on Alibaba. Here's how to tell the difference — with the numbers.

Why China, and Why Now

Let's start with the macro picture. China produced more than 70 billion garments in 2025 — over half the world's total output — and its apparel exports reached US$165.24 billion in 2024, still the largest national figure on earth, though its global clothing-export share slipped to 29.6%, the lowest since 2010 (WTO World Trade Statistics 2025, as analyzed by FASH455). ["https://www.newway-apparel.com/blog/china-garment-manufacturing-industry-statistics-factory-consolidation-2026.html","http://www.xinhuanet.com/fortune/20260901/b834d42d49f541bcad8f3b8c0de09b25/c.html"]

Two things matter for you, the buyer:

  1. The supply chain is unmatched. From yarn to finished garment, every step — knitting, dyeing, printing, sewing, labeling, packing — sits within a one-day drive in places like Zhongshan, Guangzhou and Shenzhen. This is why even Vietnam-based brands still source fabric and trims from China.
  2. The market is consolidating. The 29.6% figure is down from over 35% a decade ago because orders are moving to Southeast Asia — but mostly basic, price-sensitive commodity work. For custom, mid-range and quality-sensitive programs, China's ecosystem of fabric mills, print shops and pattern makers is still the safest bet.

Minimum order quantity (MOQ) tells you more about a factory than any glossy website. According to Alibaba's 2026 OEM t-shirt cost guide, typical MOQs by region are:

Read that table carefully. The factories advertising "MOQ 1" on Alibaba are almost always trading companies reselling stock garments, not manufacturers doing true OEM. If you want a custom color, custom print and custom label, expect a real factory to ask for 100–500 pieces per design. Below that, you're paying a small-lot premium.

The 8-Point Vetting Checklist

After 12 years in this industry, we've seen brands lose money on the wrong factory eight different ways. Run every supplier through these eight questions:

1. Are they a factory or a trading company?

Ask for a video walkthrough of the sewing floor. A real factory can turn on the camera and show you 20 sewing machines in 10 seconds. A trading company will send you a generic stock photo. You can also check their BV / SGS / Intertek audit report — genuine factories on Made-in-China display these publicly.

2. Where exactly are they located?

China's apparel clusters are geographically specialized. Zhongshan and Foshan in Guangdong are known for knitwear (t-shirts, hoodies, sweatpants). Guangzhou and Shenzhen handle fashion and fast turnover. Ningbo and Shanghai do shirting and outerwear. If a "t-shirt factory" is in a province with no textile cluster, ask why.

3. What does the sample process cost?

A reputable factory charges $30–100 for a sample and credits it back when you place bulk. If they refuse to charge for samples, they'll also refuse to take the sample seriously — and samples that aren't taken seriously become bulk orders with surprises.

4. What is the price ladder by quantity?

A real OEM quote comes in three or four tiers (e.g. 100–299 / 300–499 / 500+). A single flat price is either a stock tee they're reselling, or a quote designed to disappear once you say yes.

5. How do they handle defects?

Ask directly: "What happens if 5% of the order has print defects?" The best answer is a concrete policy — free remake, credit on next order, or a pre-shipment AQL 2.5 inspection. "We'll check quality" is not an answer.

6. What payment terms?

Standard for China OEM is 30% T/T deposit, 70% before shipment, FOB China. Anything that asks for 100% upfront, or only Western Union / crypto, is a red flag. Platform-secured trade (Made-in-China Trade Assurance, Alibaba Trade Assurance) adds buyer protection for an extra fee.

7. Can they show production photos during bulk?

Good factories send photos of fabric incoming, cutting, sewing and final QC. If a supplier goes silent for 30 days and then says "done", that's how you get a container of wrong-size shirts.

8. How fast do they reply?

Average response time under 4 hours during business hours (GMT+8) is the baseline for an active, export-focused factory. If they take 24+ hours on a quote request, multiply that delay by every stage of your project.

Understanding the FOB Price You're Quoted

When a factory quotes you a "FOB $8.50" t-shirt, that number is not random. It breaks down roughly like this (for a 200gsm 100% cotton tee at 500 pcs):

ComponentShare of FOB priceNotes
Fabric (knit + dye)45–55%The single biggest cost. Organic cotton adds 15–25% vs conventional.
Cutting & sewing labor20–25%Guangdong wages have risen ~6%/year; this is why basic work moves to Vietnam.
Print / embroidery8–15%Screen print is cheap at volume; DTG and embroidery cost more.
Labels, tags, packaging3–6%Custom woven labels and retail boxes add up.
Overhead + margin10–15%QC, rent, management, profit.

Industry decomposition based on Alibaba Apparel Production Cost Guide 2026 and public OEM cost breakdowns. ["https://apparel.alibaba.com/guide/t-shirt-production-cost-guide-real-2026-breakdown"]

This is why "can you make it for $3?" almost always gets a yes from the wrong supplier — they'll hit that price by switching from 200gsm to 160gsm, or from combed cotton to carded, and you won't know until the sample arrives.

Three Mistakes New Brands Make

Mistake 1: Choosing on price alone

A t-shirt that costs $2 less per unit but has a 12% defect rate will cost you far more than the $1,000 you "saved." At 500 pieces, a 12% defect rate means 60 pieces you can't sell — and angry customers.

Mistake 2: Skipping the paid sample

Sample cost is $50. Bulk cost is $4,000. Brands that skip the sample to "save time" typically spend two rounds of revisions and four extra weeks to fix what a $50 sample would have caught in 7 days.

Mistake 3: Ordering 2,000 pieces on the first run

The data is clear: new brands have high first-run markdown rates because sizing, color and print positioning are rarely right the first time. Start at 100–300 pieces, prove the product sells, then reorder.
